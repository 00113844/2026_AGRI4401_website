Part 1: What Is a Sensor? Foundational Concepts

The layer beneath every precision agriculture technology

Why Sensors Are the Foundation of Precision Agriculture

  • Sensors tell you what is there; positioning tells you where
  • A sensor converts physical or chemical properties into recordable signals
  • Without sensors, a GPS coordinate is just an empty point on a map
  • Structure: foundational concepts → sensing modalities → practical selection framework
Open by establishing sensors as the layer beneath every other precision agriculture technology covered elsewhere in this unit. Define a sensor at its simplest as a device that converts a physical or chemical property of the environment into a signal that can be recorded, transmitted, and acted upon. Preview the structure of the lecture.

Sensing as Indirect Measurement

  • Almost no agricultural sensor measures what an agronomist actually cares about directly
  • A canopy reflectance sensor measures reflected light intensity, from which crop health is inferred
  • A soil moisture probe infers moisture from a calibration relationship
  • Trade-off: direct measurement (accurate but slow and unscalable) vs. indirect sensing (fast and scalable but only as reliable as calibration)
  • Key principle: Every sensor reading is an inference, not a fact
Introduce the single most important conceptual habit for working with any sensor. Discuss the practical trade-offs between direct measurement and indirect sensing.

Spectral and Spatial Resolution

  • Spectral resolution: Number and narrowness of wavelength bands a sensor distinguishes
  • Ranges from 3-band RGB to hundreds of hyperspectral bands
  • Spatial resolution: Ground area represented by a single measurement or pixel
  • Fundamental trade-off: higher spectral resolution generally requires larger pixels (coarser spatial resolution)
  • A sensor rarely excels at both simultaneously
Define spectral resolution and spatial resolution. Explain the fundamental trade-off that shapes every sensor selection decision.

Temporal Resolution: How Often Can a Sensor Look?

  • Temporal resolution: How frequently a sensor revisits the same location
  • Ranges from continuous in-field sensors to satellite revisit cycles of several days
  • Crop stress events can develop and resolve within days
  • A sensing system that only revisits every two weeks may miss the actionable window
  • Spectral, spatial, and temporal resolution types interact and interact — you cannot maximize all three simultaneously
Define temporal resolution and explain why it matters as much as spectral or spatial resolution. Introduce the general principle that these three resolution types interact.

From Signal to Action: The Sensor Pipeline

  • Raw signal acquisition → calibration → conversion to meaningful units → contextualisation → actionable recommendation
  • Examples: fertiliser rate, irrigation trigger, targeted herbicide application
  • The value of a sensor is determined by how reliably this whole pipeline converts a signal into a correct decision
  • Not by its raw precision alone
Walk through the generic pipeline that recurs across every sensor type. Emphasize that the value of a sensor is determined by the entire decision pipeline, not just raw precision.

Part 2: Active vs Passive Sensing

And the Platform Landscape

Active vs Passive Sensing: The Core Distinction

  • Passive sensors: Measure energy that already exists in the environment
  • Reflected sunlight, emitted thermal radiation, ambient sound
  • Active sensors: Emit their own controlled signal and measure what returns
  • Laser pulses, radio/ultrasonic waves, artificial light flashes
  • This single distinction predicts much of a sensor's practical behaviour
Define the distinction that organises almost every sensor discussed in this lecture. Explain how this predicts a sensor's practical behaviour.

Passive Sensing: Strengths and Constraints

  • Strengths: Simpler optical design, lower power, can cover very large areas cheaply
  • Constraints: Performance depends entirely on ambient illumination
  • Unusable at night, degraded under cloud cover, requires radiometric correction
  • Dominant sensing category by volume of use in WA agriculture
  • More environmentally constrained than active sensing
Examine passive sensing in more depth. Discuss strengths and constraints with practical examples.

Active Sensing: Strengths and Constraints

  • Strengths: Independent from ambient lighting, known signal simplifies interpretation, direct distance measurement via time-of-flight
  • Constraints: Higher power consumption and complexity, higher unit cost, limited range than equivalent passive system
  • LiDAR, radar, ultrasonic rangefinders, active-illumination crop sensors
Examine active sensing in more depth. Discuss strengths and constraints of this modality.

Sensing Platforms: The Four Tiers

  • Satellite: Very wide area, systematic revisit, coarse spatial resolution, fixed schedule
  • Aerial: Flexible timing, finer resolution, higher per-hectare cost
  • UAV: Finest practical resolution, full timing control, limited coverage per flight
  • Proximal: Centimetre range, point-level ground truth, no inherent area coverage
Introduce the four platform tiers. Every sensing modality can, in principle, be deployed on more than one platform.

The Resolution Trade-Off: Why You Can't Have It All

  • Platform choice and resolution trade-offs are the same decision
  • Satellite achieves wide coverage by sacrificing spatial detail and timing flexibility
  • UAV achieves fine detail and timing control by sacrificing coverage area per flight
  • No platform-sensor combination maximizes spectral, spatial, and temporal resolution simultaneously across a large area
  • Sensor selection = identifying which resolution types matter, accepting sacrifices in others
Synthesize the resolution trade-offs into a single practical principle. This is the foundation for later sensor selection decisions.

Part 3: Time of Flight, Sonar, and Radar

Ranging Sensors

The Time-of-Flight Principle

  • Core idea: Emit a signal (light, sound, or radio waves), measure elapsed time until return
  • Distance = (signal speed × elapsed time) ÷ 2 (accounting for round trip)
  • Phase-based alternative: Measure phase shift between emitted and returned continuous wave
  • Mathematically equivalent to fractional time delay
  • Underlies ultrasonic rangefinders, radar level sensors, and LiDAR
Introduce Time of Flight as the foundational active-sensing principle. Explain both pulse-based and phase-based implementations.

Ultrasonic (Sonar) Sensing in Agriculture

  • Time-of-Flight applied using sound waves, typically 40–200 kHz band
  • Key application: Automatic spray boom height control
  • Maintains constant target height across undulating paddocks, avoiding boom strikes
  • Secondary application: Non-contact level sensing in tanks and storage bins
  • Inexpensive and robust, but limited range (~few metres) and affected by dust, foam, strong wind
Explain ultrasonic sensing with emphasis on WA agricultural applications. Discuss strengths and limitations.

Radar Sensing in Agriculture

  • Time-of-Flight using radio-frequency electromagnetic waves
  • Longer range and better penetration through dust, fog, foam than ultrasonic
  • Mature application: Non-contact radar level sensing in grain silos and storage tanks
  • Emerging applications: Soil moisture profiling, canopy structure sensing
  • Can operate through cloud, dust, and smoke (unlike optical and LiDAR)
Explain radar sensing with emphasis on agricultural applications. Introduce emerging research applications relevant to WA.

From Single-Point Ranging to 3D: The Bridge to LiDAR

  • So far: measuring distance to single point or small number of points
  • Adequate for boom height or tank level
  • Scanning ToF laser rapidly across many angles builds a 3D "point cloud"
  • This is LiDAR: A very fast, precisely-aimed ToF rangefinder repeated tens to hundreds of thousands of times per second
  • LiDAR is ToF's most information-rich application, not a separate technology
Preview the conceptual link to LiDAR. Frame it as the logical extension of time-of-flight principles.

Part 4: The Physics of Optical Sensing

Spectroscopy Fundamentals

Light Interacting with Matter

  • Spectroscopy: Study of how light interacts with matter as a function of wavelength
  • Physical foundation for every optical sensor in this unit
  • Light striking material is absorbed, reflected, or transmitted in wavelength-dependent proportions
  • Beer-Lambert relationship: Absorption increases with absorbing substance concentration and distance travelled through it
  • Underlies every spectroscopic calibration discussed later
Introduce spectroscopy and establish it as the physical foundation for optical sensing. Explain the Beer-Lambert relationship at conceptual level.

Spectral Signatures: Material Fingerprints in Light

  • Spectral signature: Characteristic pattern of absorption and reflection at different wavelengths
  • Determined by chemical bonds and molecular structure
  • Examples: Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red; water absorbs specific NIR and SWIR bands
  • Different soil minerals, organic matter, and grain proteins leave distinct absorption features
  • Allows inference of composition without direct chemical contact
Explain spectral signatures as the conceptual basis for optical sensing applications. Provide concrete examples.

Multispectral vs Hyperspectral Sensing

  • Multispectral: Few broad, well-separated bands; detects large spectral features economically
  • Blurs together finer absorption features
  • Hyperspectral: Hundreds of narrow, contiguous bands; resolves subtle absorption features
  • Substantially higher cost, larger data volume, risk of overfitting
  • Choice reflects the spectral-resolution trade-off from Part 1
Connect spectral resolution from Part 1 directly to sensor design choice. Explain practical implications of each approach.

Calibration: From Raw Spectrum to Meaningful Measurement

  • Radiometric calibration: Corrects for sensor and illumination-specific effects
  • Converts raw sensor counts to true reflectance
  • Chemometric calibration: Relates corrected spectral curve to property of interest
  • Uses reference set measured by trusted direct method (e.g., laboratory wet chemistry)
  • Practical lesson: Accuracy only as good as calibration data set; may not transfer to different soil, crop, or season
Explain why raw spectral measurement is not, by itself, useful. Discuss radiometric and chemometric calibration approaches.

Physics to Application: Soil and Vegetation Spectroscopy

  • Soil spectroscopy (Part 6): Applies spectral signature and calibration principles to soil properties
  • Non-destructive estimation at far lower cost than wet-chemistry laboratory analysis
  • Vegetation spectroscopy (Crop Spatial Variability chapter): Applies same physics to crop monitoring
  • Everything from Part 4 is the shared physical and statistical foundation
  • Developed separately for soil and crop targets
Explicitly bridge physics to major application domains. Emphasize shared foundation while acknowledging separate development paths.

Part 5: Radiative Transfer Models

Simulating Light-Vegetation Interaction

Radiative Transfer Models (RTMs): Simulating Light

  • RTM: Physics-based simulation predicting reflectance from specified biophysical inputs
  • Forward problem: Given leaf/canopy properties, predict reflectance
  • Inverse problem: Given measured reflectance, estimate properties (addressed later)
  • Generates large, physically consistent training data sets spanning conditions impractical to measure
  • Tests sensitivity of spectral indices before deploying real sensors
  • PROSAIL: Most widely used vegetation RTM (PROSPECT + SAIL)
Introduce RTMs as physics-based simulations. Explain value of forward simulation capability. Introduce PROSAIL framework.

PROSPECT: Leaf Optical Model

  • Treats leaf as layered optical system
  • Characterized by key absorbing constituents: chlorophyll, water, dry matter, carotenoids, anthocyanins
  • Calculates leaf reflectance and transmittance across full optical spectrum
  • Based on known absorption coefficients and internal leaf tissue scattering structure
  • Value: Ask "what reflectance if only chlorophyll changed?" — impossible experimentally
Explain PROSPECT leaf optical model in conceptual terms. Emphasize ability to isolate effects of individual properties.

SAIL: Canopy Optical Model

  • Scales from leaf (PROSPECT) to canopy level
  • Treats canopy as layer of many leaves characterized by LAI and Leaf Angle Distribution
  • Simulates light scattering between leaf layers and soil background
  • Explicit treatment of viewing and illumination geometry
  • Reproduces bidirectional reflectance effects as direct output, not empirical correction
Explain SAIL canopy model as the scaling step. Emphasize how it treats viewing geometry explicitly.

Forward Simulation and Inverse Retrieval

  • Forward simulation: PROSAIL from known biophysical inputs to predicted reflectance
  • Inverse retrieval: Given measured reflectance, search for PROSAIL inputs that produced it
  • Fundamental difficulty: Multiple input combinations can produce similar reflectance
  • Equifinality: Same observed spectrum, multiple plausible answers
  • Explains why spectral estimation always has inherent ambiguity
Introduce inverse problem explicitly. Explain equifinality from first principles. This is foundational to understanding spectral index limitations.

Part 6: Soil Spectroscopy and Digital Soil Mapping

Why Soil Has a Spectral Signature Too

  • Soil organic matter darkens reflectance broadly across visible spectrum
  • Clay minerals (kaolinite, smectite) produce absorption features in SWIR
  • Iron oxides (common in WA lateritic soils) produce characteristic visible and NIR features
  • Correlates with phosphorus-fixing behaviour
  • Implication: Soil spectral curve carries genuine information about mineralogy, organic matter, texture
Extend spectral signature concept to soil. Use WA-specific examples to ground the material.

Digital Soil Mapping (DSM)

  • DSM: Predict soil properties continuously across landscape from limited point observations
  • Combines point measurements with spatially complete covariate layers
  • Covariates: terrain attributes (elevation, slope), electrical conductivity, satellite/aerial imagery
  • Spectroscopy contributes two ways:
  • 1. Directly: spectrally predicts soil property at each sampled point (faster, cheaper than wet chemistry)
  • 2. Indirectly: provides spectral covariate layers for statistical interpolation
Define digital soil mapping. Explain how spectroscopy contributes to DSM workflows.

NIR Benchtop Spectroscopy: Laboratory Workhorse

  • Most mature, widely deployed spectroscopic application in agriculture
  • Soil/grain/plant tissue sample scanned in laboratory instrument across NIR range
  • Spectrum converted to property estimate using chemometric calibration
  • Properties: soil organic carbon, clay content, grain protein/moisture
  • Advantage: Seconds to minutes vs hours/days for wet chemistry, minimal sample prep
  • Cost: Requires large, representative calibration data set
Explain NIR benchtop spectroscopy. Discuss practical advantages and calibration requirements.

Field Soil Spectroscopy

  • Extends NIR spectroscopy from controlled laboratory to field environment
  • Proximal: Mounts NIR sensor on vehicle or handheld probe
  • Scans soil directly in paddock (surface or via penetrating probe)
  • Trade-offs: Trades precision and environmental control for dense, georeferenced data sets
  • Enables spatially continuous digital soil mapping (lab NIR alone produces point values only)
  • Affected by field moisture, temperature, ambient light
Explain proximal soil spectroscopy and its role in enabling digital soil mapping at paddock scale.

WA Application: Soil Spectroscopy for Carbon, Constraints, and Texture

  • Soil organic carbon mapping: Supports productivity and carbon credit schemes
  • Cost-effective across WA's large broadacre holdings
  • Texture and clay mapping: Supports toposequence interpretation, predicts CEC and P-fixing behaviour
  • Subsoil constraint mapping: Sodicity, acidity, constraining layer depth
  • Informs targeted amendment decisions (lime, gypsum)
  • Key insight: Makes paddock-scale soil chemistry affordable in WA

Part 7: Computer Vision for Weed Management

Two Philosophies of Weed Detection

  • Green-on-brown: Detecting green plant against bare soil, dead vegetation, or stubble
  • Scenario: chemical fallow or pre-emergent paddock with no living crop
  • Any green signal = weed by definition
  • Green-on-green: Detecting weed among live, green crop plants of similar colour
  • Scenario: established, growing crop
  • Colour alone cannot distinguish weed from crop
  • Require entirely different sensing approaches
Introduce the two fundamentally different weed-detection problems. Establish that they require entirely different solutions.

Green-on-Brown: Colour-Based Discrimination

  • Comparatively simple optical sensing problem solvable with reflectance contrast
  • Measure reflectance in red and near-infrared (vegetation index basis)
  • Reliably distinguish live green vegetation from bare soil or dead plant material
  • No need to identify specific weed species
  • Commercial deployment: Spot-spray systems on boom sprayers
  • Each nozzle triggered only where green-vegetation signal detected
  • WA application: chemical fallow and summer weed control programs
  • Weed cover typically << full paddock area, substantial herbicide cost saving
Explain green-on-brown detection. Emphasize simplicity and cost effectiveness for WA applications.

Green-on-Green: Computer Vision and Deep Learning

  • Fundamentally more sophisticated approach required
  • Weed and crop both green, both strongly reflect NIR light
  • Reflectance-based discrimination provides essentially no useful signal
  • Modern approach: Computer vision and deep learning (convolutional neural networks)
  • Trained on large, labelled image data sets
  • Learns subtler visual cues: leaf shape, texture, growth habit, spatial arrangement
  • Challenges: Computationally demanding, requires real-time inference, crop/weed-specific training needed
Explain why green-on-green requires deep learning. Discuss computational and training requirements.

Targeted Spot-Spraying: Economics and Resistance Management

  • Sensor signal must trigger individual nozzle/section with fast response latency
  • Economic case (WA): Chemical fallow paddocks with <50% weed cover
  • 50–90% herbicide volume reductions compared to blanket spraying
  • Payback within 1–3 seasons on broadacre WA operation
  • Herbicide resistance management: Targeted full-rate application vs blanket sub-lethal exposure
  • Reduces selection pressure driving resistance evolution

Part 8: Thermal Sensing

The Physics of Thermal Sensing

  • Thermal sensing: Passive modality measuring emitted infrared radiation
  • Converts to surface temperature estimate via temperature-radiation relationship
  • Key physiological link: Well-watered, transpiring leaf cools through evaporative latent heat
  • Typically runs several degrees cooler than air temperature
  • Water stress: Closes stomata, reduces evaporative cooling, canopy temperature rises
  • Single mechanism underlies all thermal sensing applications in this Part
Explain thermal sensing as passive infrared measurement. Establish the physiological basis for agronomic interpretation.

Thermal Imaging for Plant Water Status and Irrigation

  • Crop Water Stress Index (CWSI): Standard derived metric
  • Normalizes canopy temperature against fully-watered and fully-stressed references
  • More robust to changing ambient conditions than raw canopy temperature
  • Application: Handheld sensors, UAV thermal cameras, fixed in-field thermal sensors
  • Detect water stress onset before visible wilting
  • Map spatial pattern of water stress across field
  • Relevant to WA: Irrigated horticultural and pasture systems (clearer payoff than dryland broadacre)
Develop water-status application from physiology. Explain CWSI metric and WA-relevant applications.

Thermal Sensing in Crop Breeding

  • Research and breeding application: Screening for drought/heat tolerance at scale
  • Manual physiological measurement (leaf gas exchange, water potential) is too slow
  • UAV thermal cameras: Flown across breeding trial in single flight
  • Provides canopy temperature value for every plot simultaneously
  • Allows ranking of genotypes by stomatal regulation and water-use behaviour
  • High-throughput phenotyping: Relevant to WA wheat and canola breeding for terminal drought
Explain crop breeding application as high-throughput phenotyping. Connect to WA breeding goals for drought tolerance.

Thermal Sensing Beyond Water Status

  • Frost risk and damage assessment: Detects characteristic frost-affected temperature signature
  • Maps topographically controlled cold-air drainage pattern
  • Visualizes frost risk concentration before damage occurs
  • Disease and pest detection: Some alter canopy transpiration and temperature before visible symptoms
  • Offers early warning layer (with caution about equifinality)
  • Complements spectral and visual detection methods
Extend thermal sensing beyond water status. Emphasize need for ground-truthing due to equifinality.

Part 9: LiDAR and 3D Structural Sensing

LiDAR: Time-of-Flight at Laser Speed

  • LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) = ToF principle with laser, scanned rapidly across many angles
  • Emits tens to hundreds of thousands of laser pulses per second
  • Precisely times return of each pulse to calculate distance
  • Records angle at which each pulse was emitted
  • Combined with GNSS/IMU position and orientation
  • Builds dense 3D point cloud (potentially millions of points per hectare)
Connect directly back to Part 3. Explain LiDAR as rapid scanning time-of-flight implementation.

LiDAR for Digital Terrain Models

  • Most mature LiDAR application in agriculture: high-accuracy Digital Terrain Models (DTMs)
  • Single LiDAR pulse produces multiple returns through vegetation canopy gaps
  • Bare-earth filtering algorithms separate "last return" (ground) from "first return" (canopy)
  • Reconstructs accurate terrain surface even beneath substantial vegetation
  • Advantage vs optical imagery: Works through vegetation cover
  • Supports terrain-driven spatial pattern analysis: frost drainage basins, waterlogging zones, erosion paths
Explain most mature LiDAR application. Emphasize advantage over passive optical for terrain mapping.

LiDAR for Crop Height and Canopy Structure

  • Extends LiDAR from bare-earth to vegetation analysis
  • Retain and analyse canopy returns (don't filter them out)
  • Crop height: Vertical difference between canopy-return and bare-earth points
  • Canopy structure metrics: Density at different height layers, gaps, row geometry
  • 3D detail exceeds 2D optical imagery projection of canopy surface
  • Provides direct structural measurement where optical vegetation indices only offer indirect spectral proxy
Explain LiDAR application to vegetation. Emphasize 3D measurement advantages over 2D optical approaches.

LiDAR-Based Biomass Estimation

  • Calibrate statistical relationship between LiDAR-derived metrics and destructively-harvested biomass
  • Parallels calibration logic from spectroscopy (Part 4)
  • LiDAR strength: Measures structure directly, not inferring from reflected light
  • Far less susceptible to soil-background and saturation confounds limiting spectral estimation
  • LiDAR limitations: Substantially more expensive than multispectral camera
  • Computationally demanding point cloud processing
  • Accuracy only as good as reference biomass samples used for calibration
Explain LiDAR biomass estimation. Discuss genuine strengths relative to spectral methods and practical limitations.

Part 10: Mechanical and Force Sensors

Downforce Sensors: Measuring Seeder Force

  • Sensing modality outside optical and ranging families
  • Measures mechanical force (load cell or hydraulic pressure transducer)
  • Mounted on seeder opener linkage: Measures actual force applied to soil in real time
  • Addresses key problem: Force needed for consistent seed depth changes with soil conditions
  • Gravel rise → sandy slope → clay valley within single pass
  • Sensor-fed control: Automatically adjusts downforce row-by-row in real time
  • Fixed mechanical settings cannot achieve this
Introduce downforce sensors as mechanical sensing modality. Explain problem they solve for seeding.

Downforce Matters: Seed-to-Soil Contact Loop

  • Seed-to-soil contact = critical, largely invisible variable for germination
  • Insufficient downforce: Seed in open furrow, poor soil contact, air pockets
  • Excessive downforce: Smearing and compaction restrict root development
  • Automatic sensor-fed control: Closes loop in real time, row-by-row
  • Reduces mechanically-driven component of establishment-stage spatial variability
  • Fixed mechanical settings set for assumed "average" condition cannot achieve this
Connect downforce sensing to establishment physiology. Explain how it closes the seed-to-soil contact loop.

Other Mechanical and Force Sensors

  • Draft force sensors: Measure pulling force through implement
  • Tillage resistance mapping (direct detection of compacted zones, hard-pans)
  • Tractor performance and fuel-efficiency monitoring
  • Soil penetrometers/cone penetrometers: Measure soil mechanical resistance with depth
  • Identify compaction layers and hard-pans
  • Pairs naturally with Controlled Traffic Farming verification
  • Standard ground-truthing method for CTF compaction-reduction benefits
Survey broader family of mechanical and force sensors. Emphasize application to CTF and soil compaction assessment.

Weather Stations: The Underpinning Sensor Network

Common Sensor Selection Mistakes

  • Pitfall 1: Choose sensor based on headline resolution without checking temporal resolution matches how fast condition develops
  • Pitfall 2: Deploy passive optical sensor in conditions (cloud, low sun, dust) where accuracy was never validated
  • Pitfall 3: Apply spectroscopic/computer-vision calibration from one soil/crop/weed flora to another without retraining
  • Pitfall 4: Treat single sensor reading as fact, forgetting indirect-measurement principle
  • Miss the ground-truthing step that every chapter emphasizes
