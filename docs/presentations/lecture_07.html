AGRI4401: Soil and Water Spatial Variability

Part 1: The Scales of Variability

Why crops are never uniform

Why Crops Are Never Uniform

  • Even a uniformly sown paddock shows 2–3 t·ha⁻¹ yield variation across a few hundred metres
  • This variability is not random but nested outcomes of processes at different scales:
  • Seed-soil microsite (cm): Moisture, contact, temperature for germination
  • Individual plant (dm): Tillers, leaf area, light capture
  • Canopy community (m): Competition for light, water, nutrients
  • Paddock/landscape (ha): Soil type, terrain, management history
  • Each scale has different drivers and demands different sensing tools
Open with a provocation: establish that variability is not random but nested. Use a "zoom out" framing device: seed → plant → quadrat → paddock → farm. This organizing idea structures the entire lecture.

A Tale of Three Zones: The Wongan Hills Example

  • The Gravelly Rise: Shallow, water-repellent topsoil, P-fixing, consistently lowest-yielding
  • The Sandplain Mid-Slope: Deep sand, free-draining, prone to early N leaching and terminal drought stress
  • The Clay Valley Floor: High water-holding capacity, but prone to waterlogging and seasonal salinity
  • The SAME uniform management produces 2–3× yield range across these three zones
  • Key question: How do we detect, diagnose, and respond to this variability?
Ground the scale concept in a concrete, recurring example. Show how identical management produces vastly different outcomes across landscape positions.

The Precision Agriculture Toolkit

  • Value chain: Detect → Quantify → Diagnose → Respond
  • Agronomic scouting: Ground truth, oldest and most reliable layer
  • Biophysical/biochemical sensing: LAI, chlorophyll, fluorescence — direct physiological measurement
  • Spectral remote sensing: Proximal, UAV, satellite — scale physiological inference
  • Yield monitoring: Close the loop, measure the final outcome
  • Key principle: No single layer sufficient alone — equifinality means different causes produce the same signal
  • Only triangulating across layers resolves ambiguity

Part 2: Establishment — The Seed-Soil Interface

From seed to seedling: where variability begins

From Seed to Seedling: Germination Physiology

  • Imbibition: Seed must absorb sufficient soil water to activate enzymes and begin metabolism
  • Without sustained moisture, germination stalls
  • Thermal time: Once imbibed, germination rate governed by growth-degree-days (GDD)
  • Not calendar days — same crop sown 3 weeks apart reaches same stage at different dates
  • WA complication: Dry sowing ahead of season break common practice
  • Seeds sit dormant weeks until rainfall trigger → patchy emergence timing within single paddock
  • Spatial variability exists before first leaf emerges
Explain the physiological sequence determining germination success. Emphasize WA-specific complications with dry sowing that create emergence variability.

The Seeding System: Mechanical Variability

  • Furrow opening: Knife points vs disc openers — each handles soil conditions differently
  • Seed metering/singulation: Precision of individual seed release and spacing
  • Seed depth and orientation: Consistency affects emergence timing; orientation affects early leaf geometry
  • Furrow closing: Press wheels re-firm soil around seed
  • Critical invisible variable: Seed-to-soil contact — air pockets delay or prevent imbibition
  • WA complication: Water-repellent ("non-wetting") sands cause irrigation/rainfall to channel preferentially along furrows
  • Adds soil-chemistry-driven establishment variability on top of mechanical variability
Break down seeding system as chain of mechanical decisions. Introduce seed-to-soil contact as the critical factor that creates establishment variability.

Crop Stand Evaluation: Counting What Went Wrong

  • Standard diagnostic: Walk representative row, count 100 consecutive plant stations
  • Tally: skips (no plant), doubles (two plants), late emergers (behind cohort)
  • Failure mode causes:
  • — Skips at fixed intervals → blocked tube or metering fault (equipment)
  • — Skips scattered randomly → poor contact or non-wetting soil
  • — Doubles → poor singulation at seed meter
  • — Late emergers in patches → variable depth or localised moisture deficit
  • Target plant population WA wheat: ~150–180 plants·m⁻²
  • Determine if stand adequate or remedial re-sowing warranted
Present standard field diagnostic. Each failure mode points to different upstream cause, guiding investigation and remedial action.

Seeding Technology: Closing the Gap

  • NIR seed sensors: Monitor seed flow real-time, flag singulation faults as they occur
  • In-cab correction during seeding, not discovery weeks later
  • Closing-wheel force control: Automatically adjust down-pressure as soil type changes within pass
  • Critical on paddocks crossing gravel, sand, clay zones in metres
  • Air-seeder metering improvements: Brush/finger-style precision meters reduce seed damage
  • Improve singulation consistency at higher ground speeds
  • Direct engineering answer to diagnostic categories from previous slide

Part 3: Crop Development and Canopy Construction

Staging growth, scaling from leaf to canopy

Staging the Crop: Zadoks Scale and Thermal Time

  • Zadoks decimal code (0–99): Standard framework for cereal growth stages
  • Germination (0) → Tillering (2x) → Stem elongation (3x) → Booting (4x) → Heading (5x) → Anthesis (6x) → Grain fill/ripening (7x–9x)
  • Why thermal time, not calendar days? WA season break varies by 4–6 weeks between years
  • Calendar predictions of flowering/maturity unreliable
  • Thermal-time models transfer well across seasons and locations
  • Backbone connecting establishment timing to every later developmental milestone
Introduce Zadoks scale and explain why thermal time is superior to calendar days for WA conditions. This framework connects establishment to crop monitoring.

The Phyllochron: Leaf Appearance Timing

  • Phyllochron: Thermal time interval between successive main stem leaf appearances
  • Relatively stable for given genotype but shifts with vernalisation and photoperiod
  • Differs across WA wheat and barley varieties in different rainfall zones
  • Operational importance: Sets pace of canopy expansion and leaf area development
  • Defines developmental window for tiller bud initiation at each leaf node
  • Short phyllochron: Reaches canopy closure faster, useful for early weed suppression
  • But also faster early-season water use
Define phyllochron and explain operational significance. Links leaf development to canopy closure and competitiveness.

Seed Orientation and Early Light Capture

  • Connect seed mechanics (Part 2) to canopy-level light capture
  • Consistent orientation: Radicle down, coleoptile up
  • Produces predictable vertical emergence, consistent early leaf azimuth
  • Inconsistent orientation: Variable leaf angle and clumping at emergence
  • Reduces early radiation interception efficiency when canopy most exposed
  • Highest soil evaporation losses at this stage
  • Weed competition consequence: Later or uneven canopy closure leaves wider light-gap window
  • Weed seedlings can establish during this window
Connect seed-level mechanics to canopy-level outcomes. Explain compounding effects on weed competition.

Tillering: The Plant's Strategy for Filling Space

  • Tiller bud initiation: Occurs at base of each leaf node
  • Bud outgrowth regulated by: Light quality (low R:FR ratio suppresses), N availability, assimilate availability
  • WA reality: Typically only 2–3 tillers plus main stem produce heads
  • Later tillers frequently abort under terminal drought stress
  • Critical implication: Early-season plant density and vigour largely determine head-bearing structure capacity
  • Poor establishment cannot reliably "tiller its way" to yield potential if water runs out before later tillers mature
Explain tillering physiology and WA-specific limitations under water stress. Links establishment to yield potential.

The Yield Component Chain

  • Multiplicative sequence:
  • Plant population × Tillers per plant × Spikelets per spike × Grains per spikelet × Grain weight = Yield
  • Key implication: Error or stress at any single link propagates through every downstream link
  • Partial compensation between links common but rarely complete
  • Water-limited WA spring: Compensating process (extra tillering) may lack resources
  • Conceptual bridge: Establishment problem (Part 2) reappears as yield-map low zone at harvest (Part 8)
  • Even though measurements taken months apart

Part 4: Quantifying the Canopy

LAI, light interception, and physiological status

Leaf Area Index: The Crop's Solar Panel Area

  • LAI: Total one-sided leaf area per unit ground area (dimensionless)
  • Determines how much incoming radiation the canopy intercepts
  • Optimal LAI trajectory, not "more is always better":
  • — Too low: radiation reaches bare soil, wasted on evaporation and weed seedlings
  • — Too high: crop accumulates luxury leaf area, consuming water needed for grain fill
  • WA Mediterranean pattern: Sharp spring rainfall decline terminates grain fill
  • Crop with excessive winter LAI runs short of water exactly when grain fill peaks
  • LAI is the central biophysical variable Parts 4–6 attempt to estimate
Define LAI and explain why optimal trajectory matters under WA conditions. Set up LAI as central theme for rest of Part 4.

Measuring Light Interception

  • Direct instrumented measurement:
  • Ceptometers, plant canopy analysers (LI-COR LAI-2000/2200)
  • Measure gap fraction or intercepted PAR beneath canopy
  • Invert light-transmission model to estimate LAI
  • Accurate but point measurement requiring field instrument and operator
  • Indirect spectral inference:
  • Vegetation indices correlate with LAI and fractional cover
  • Scale across paddock from single sensor pass
  • Indirect proxy, not direct physical measurement
  • Trade-off: Ground instruments = trustworthy points; spectral proxies = scalable maps
  • NDVI saturation at moderate-to-high LAI motivates soil-adjusted indices
Contrast direct and indirect approaches. Emphasize trade-offs and technical limitations of each method.

Radiation Use Efficiency: Light to Biomass

  • RUE: Biomass accumulated per unit intercepted PAR (g·MJ⁻¹)
  • Relatively conservative within species under non-stressed conditions
  • Biomass accumulation approximately linear with cumulative intercepted radiation
  • Agronomic value: RUE drops measurably under water deficit, nutrient deficiency, disease
  • Drops even before visible symptoms appear
  • Falling RUE trend: More physiologically direct stress indicator than simple greenness index
  • Greenness can remain unchanged while RUE already declining
Explain RUE concept and its value as stress indicator. Link to earlier spectroscopy discussion.

Proximal Sensing of Physiological Status

  • SPAD meters: Transmittance-based proxy for leaf chlorophyll concentration
  • Rapid, low-cost nitrogen status indicator
  • PhotosynQ MultispeQ: Comprehensive field instrument
  • Chlorophyll fluorescence (photosystem II efficiency)
  • Reflectance-based indices for richer physiological readout
  • Photochemical Reflectance Index (PRI): Narrow-band index sensitive to xanthophyll cycle pigments
  • Proxy for photosynthetic light-use efficiency and photoprotective response
  • Detects stress response within hours, before chlorophyll loss or SPAD visible
Survey proximal sensing tools for physiological assessment. Emphasize speed of detection and integration of multiple parameters.

Beyond Green: Biochemical and Biophysical Properties

  • Properties to measure:
  • LAI, chlorophyll content, anthocyanin content (stress/cold-induced)
  • Solar-induced fluorescence (SIF): Direct photosynthetic activity proxy
  • Increasingly retrievable from hyperspectral and satellite sensors
  • Near-direct proxy rather than indirect structural proxy
  • Grain quality traits: Protein, oil, starch content
  • Matter as much to WA profitability as yield given protein-based grading
  • Frame this as the "shopping list" of what sensing tries to measure
Broaden inventory of plant properties that precision sensing aims to retrieve. Set up physics of measurement in Part 5.

Canopy Architecture: Why Leaf Angle Matters

  • Leaf Angle Distribution (LAD): Major determinant of light distribution through canopy depth
  • Erectophile (near-vertical leaves): Light penetrates deeper, supports higher optimal LAI
  • Typical of modern cereal varieties, relevant to WA dense canopies
  • Planophile/plagiophile (horizontal leaves): Efficient top absorption but severe lower shading
  • "Chlorophyll sieving": Upper leaves filter light before reaching lower layers
  • Photoprotective traits: Carotenoids, anthocyanins dissipate excess energy
  • Waxy cuticles reduce radiation absorption and water loss under high spring radiation
  • Each trait implicitly recognized by spectral sensors
Explain canopy architecture effects on light distribution. Connect to photoprotective mechanisms. Sets stage for Part 5 physics.

Part 5: The Physics of Spectral Sensing

From photons to pixel values

Irradiance, Radiance, Reflectance: Terminology

  • Irradiance: Incoming radiant flux per unit area (W·m⁻²)
  • Varies with sun angle, time of day, atmospheric conditions
  • Radiance: Flux per unit area *leaving* surface toward sensor
  • What the sensor actually records
  • Reflectance: Ratio of reflected to incident radiation
  • The intrinsic surface property agronomists actually want
  • Obtained only after correcting radiance for illumination and atmospheric conditions
  • Critical point: Raw radiance from two flights, times, or satellite overpasses NOT directly comparable
  • Must convert to reflectance — often skipped in casual drone imagery use
  • Spurious "change" may just be change in illumination
Establish precise vocabulary before vegetation indices can be interpreted correctly. Stress importance of radiometric correction.

How Vegetation Interacts with Light

  • Leaf-level optical behaviour: Foundation of all vegetation remote sensing
  • Absorption: Chlorophyll in blue and red, water in specific NIR and SWIR bands
  • Transmittance: Some light passes through thin leaf tissue, especially NIR
  • Scattering: At internal cell walls and air-space interfaces
  • Produces characteristic high-reflectance NIR "plateau"
  • Underlies almost every vegetation index in use
  • Canopy-level complications:
  • — Multiple scattering between overlapping leaf layers
  • — Soil background reflectance at low LAI (early-season confound)
  • — Bidirectional reflectance (BRDF) — same canopy appears different depending on viewing/illumination geometry
Explain leaf and canopy optical behavior. Introduce BRDF concept and its operational implications.

The Spectral Regions: Visible to SWIR

  • Visible (400–700 nm): Chlorophyll absorption dominates, blue and red
  • Produces "green hump" reflectance peak
  • Red band most stress-sensitive in visible region
  • Near-infrared (700–1300 nm): High-reflectance plateau from internal scattering
  • Strongly sensitive to LAI, canopy structure, biomass
  • Red edge (680–750 nm) steep transition — sensitive to chlorophyll and early stress
  • Valuable for nitrogen status assessment
  • Shortwave infrared (1300–2500 nm): Dominated by leaf and canopy water content
  • Distinct absorption features near 1450 nm and 1940 nm
  • Features sensitive to lignin, cellulose, protein — basis for grain quality trait estimation
Survey three spectral regions and their agronomic significance. Emphasize specific features relevant to WA crops.

Why Spectral Models Are Hard: Equifinality and Hughes

  • Equifinality: Different combinations of LAI, chlorophyll, soil background produce statistically indistinguishable spectra
  • Single index value cannot uniquely identify which biophysical driver produced it
  • Hughes phenomenon: "Curse of dimensionality" in hyperspectral calibration
  • Adding more spectral bands beyond optimal number can make prediction *worse*, not better
  • Especially with limited field samples
  • Practical consequence: High R² in single-season, single-site paper does NOT guarantee transfer reliability
  • Accuracy depends on different soil type, season, growth stage
  • Central "buyer beware" lesson: Before trusting an index, ask: validated where, on what, with what RMSE?
Introduce two critical caveats. Explain equifinality and Hughes phenomenon in operational terms. This is central caution throughout lecture.

Part 6: From Spectra to Insight

Vegetation indices, time series, and applications

Vegetation Indices: Compressing Information

  • NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index): (NIR – Red) / (NIR + Red)
  • Simple, robust, by far most widely used
  • Saturates at moderate-to-high LAI (≈3–4) and sensitive to soil background at low cover
  • SAVI (Soil-Adjusted Vegetation Index): Introduces soil brightness correction factor
  • Reduces background sensitivity
  • OSAVI (Optimized SAVI): Tunes correction empirically across range of soils
  • No site-specific calibration needed
  • Red-edge indices (NDRE): More sensitive at higher biomass than NDVI
  • Better for detecting in-season nitrogen status
  • Directly relevant to variable-rate topdressing on WA cereals
Introduce most widely used vegetation indices. Explain strengths, limitations, and WA-relevant applications of each.

Reading the Crop Through Time: Time Series

  • Single-date index value: Far less informative than trajectory across season
  • Curve shape encodes: Rate of green-up, timing and magnitude of peak, rate of senescence
  • Each captures developmental and stress information no snapshot can
  • Phenological metrics:
  • — Green-up date
  • — Peak index value
  • — Integrated (cumulative) index as proxy for total intercepted radiation and biomass
  • Critical point: WA season break varies by weeks between years
  • Meaningful comparison must reference thermal time or growth stage, not calendar date
  • Otherwise apparent "differences" may simply reflect different sowing dates
Argue for time-series approach. Emphasize importance of thermal-time normalization for WA conditions.

From Time Series to Decisions

  • Stress detection: When zone trajectory diverges from its own pattern or paddock average
  • Flags emerging problem before visible to naked eye
  • Gives agronomist head start on field investigation
  • Yield prediction: Integrated or peak-season index values correlate with final yield
  • Supports in-season yield forecasting
  • Valuable for marketing and harvest logistics planning
  • Equifinality reminder: Stress signal tells WHERE and roughly WHEN problem emerging
  • Rarely tells WHY without ground-truthing visit
  • Directly motivates diagnostic framework in Part 7

Monitoring Operationally: Nested Measurement Layers

  • Triangulate across nested layers:
  • Proximal sensors (SPAD, PhotosynQ): Rapid trusted spot-checks at specific points
  • UAV multispectral/hyperspectral: Paddock-scale mapping at flexible flight time
  • Satellite time series: Full-season context and multi-year history
  • Equifinality strategy: No single layer trusted in isolation
  • Satellite-flagged anomaly checked against UAV for finer detail
  • Both confirmed or explained by proximal or ground measurement
  • Nested, cross-validating workflow is practical answer to Part 5 cautions

Case Study: Spectral Biomass Estimation Accuracy Ceiling

  • WA perennial ryegrass pastures: Published benchmark calibration
  • Spectral indices calibrated against ground-truth biomass cuts
  • Typical RMSE: ~490 kg DM·ha⁻¹
  • Useful, genuinely actionable for paddock-scale grazing decisions
  • Well short of precision for subtle, early-stage stress at individual-plant scale
  • Key lesson: Spectral biomass estimation is useful tool with known, non-zero error ceiling
  • Not a perfect measurement
  • Always ask: What RMSE has this index-biomass relationship been validated against before trusting it?

Part 7: Diagnosing Spatial Patterns

A field framework for identifying causes

Reading Patterns: A Diagnostic Framework

  • Four diagnostic questions:
  • 1. Geometry: Linear/rectilinear (equipment or management) vs circular/patchy (biological or soil)?
  • 2. Location: At border, rise, or low point?
  • 3. Persistence: Same place year-to-year (soil/terrain cause) or appear/disappear randomly (weather/pest/management)?
  • 4. Timing: Early (establishment/equipment), mid-season (nutrition/disease/water), or late (senescence/disease/lodging)?
  • Next six slides apply framework to specific cause classes
  • Final synthesis ties into single decision tree
Introduce systematic framework before assigning causes. Establish four-question structure organizing rest of Part 7.

Equipment-Induced Patterns

  • Tell-tale signature: Perfectly straight edges with repeat spacing matching equipment width
  • Examples:
  • — Blocked fertiliser/seed tube → narrow linear gaps at outlet spacing
  • — Tank-mix residual burn-down → sharp-edged patches matching spray pass boundaries
  • — Wheel-track compaction → parallel lines at tractor tramline spacing
  • — Boom section overlap/skip → linear strips at section width
  • Why easiest category to confirm: Geometrically rigid, tied to specific machine dimension
  • Often resolved by checking machine width or pass log
  • No paddock digging needed
Apply diagnostic framework to equipment causes. Emphasize ease of verification through machine records.

Soil-Driven Patterns: Fixed Properties

  • Rooted in fixed soil properties: Persistent across seasons, correlate with soil maps
  • WA examples:
  • — Water-repellent sand → erratic speckled early establishment, fades later
  • — Shallow gravel/duplex on rises → chronic low vigour matching soil boundary
  • — Sodic/dispersive subsoils → restrict root depth, drought stress in dry years only
  • Diagnostic tell: Strong, persistent correlation with independent soil map
  • Regardless of season — distinguishes from terrain and abiotic patterns below
Apply framework to soil-driven causes. Explain WA-specific soil constraints and their spatial signatures.

Terrain-Driven Patterns: Topography Rules

  • Patterns governed by topography rather than soil chemistry:
  • Frost: Cold air drainage concentrates in low-lying valleys and basins
  • Increasing risk as earlier sowing exposes reproductive stages to frost-prone season
  • Waterlogging: Same low points saturate in wet years
  • Restricts root oxygen, induces nitrogen loss via denitrification
  • Erosion: Water erosion follows flow paths and contours
  • Wind erosion affects exposed sandy rises
  • Each leaves geomorphically distinct, topographically predictable signature
  • Elevation model can anticipate before it occurs
Apply framework to terrain-driven causes. Explain WA-specific frost and waterlogging risks and their predictability.

Disease Patterns: Airborne vs Soilborne

  • Two fundamentally different spatial signatures pointing to opposite responses:
  • Airborne foliar pathogens: Stripe rust, septoria (high-rainfall WA), blackleg (canola)
  • Patches *actively expand* with directional downwind bias
  • Can worsen rapidly in one season under favourable weather
  • Soilborne pathogens: Rhizoctonia bare patch (characteristic WA disease), take-all
  • Patches spatially *static*, slowly expanding year-to-year
  • Tied to paddock history (host stubble, pH, tillage)
  • Diagnostic tell: Static patch = soilborne (rotation/cultivar decisions next season)
  • Actively spreading = airborne (fungicide timing this season)
Explain two disease classes and their spatial dynamics. Emphasize different diagnostic and management implications.

Weed-Driven Patterns: Borders and Banks

  • Border concentration: Heaviest weed escapes at paddock borders
  • Reduced spray overlap, lower crop competition at edge, continuous seed introduction
  • Seed-bank patches: Discrete "hot spots" reflect locally elevated seed bank
  • From prior poor control or uncontrolled summer weed set-seed cohort
  • Near-row escapes: Weeds close to crop row harder to control
  • Seeding disturbance dilutes residual herbicide concentration where weed-crop compete most
  • Technology response: PWM spray nozzles vary duty cycle at constant pressure
  • Allow variable application rate while maintaining droplet size and spray quality
  • Critical for controlling border and patch escapes and avoiding under-dosing driving resistance
Apply framework to weed spatial ecology. Connect to herbicide resistance management and spray technology.

Abiotic Stress Patterns: WA's Three Dominants

  • Frost: Topographically controlled, increasing risk as sowing earlier
  • Terminal drought: Interacts with soil water-holding capacity
  • Sandy rises stress first and most severely — WA's central Mediterranean production constraint
  • Spring rainfall decline ends grain fill abruptly
  • Dryland salinity: Long-standing WA degradation issue
  • Saline groundwater discharges at valley floors and break-of-slope
  • Produces chronically bare patches with salt-tolerant weed margins
  • Integration point: Abiotic stress rarely sole independent cause
  • Almost always expressed through soil and topographic template
Apply framework to WA's dominant abiotic stressors. Emphasize soil-terrain template interaction.

Diagnostic Decision Tree

  • Synthesis of six cause categories anchored to four-question framework:
  • "What does pattern look like and where?" → route to cause
  • Linear geometry → equipment
  • Matches soil map → soil constraint
  • Matches elevation → frost/waterlogging/erosion
  • Expanding directionally in-season → airborne disease
  • Static, tied to host history → soilborne disease
  • At borders or discrete hot spots → weeds
  • Wet/dry year only → abiotic stress interaction
  • Capstone lesson: Real paddocks show overlapping causes
  • Ground-truthing (soil pit, disease ID, tissue test) always final necessary step
  • Equifinality caution: pattern alone, like spectra alone, suggestive but never conclusive

Part 8: Yield Monitoring and Mapping

Closing the loop with outcome measurement

Yield Monitors: Sensor Stack on the Header

  • Grain flow sensors: Optical, impact-plate, or photoelectric designs
  • Estimate mass flow rate of clean grain in elevator
  • Moisture sensors: Typically capacitance-based
  • Measure grain moisture for standard-moisture yield basis
  • GNSS position logging: Ties flow measurement to coordinate at ~1 Hz
  • Header position and width sensors: Confirm engagement and working width
  • Key conceptual point: Yield monitors *estimate* flow rate from indirect signal
  • Do not directly weigh grain
  • Calibration is not optional
Explain sensor stack and measurement principles. Stress that monitoring is estimation, not direct weighing.

Calibrating Yield Monitors

  • Multi-point calibration: Loads across representative range of flow rates and speeds
  • Not single load at one condition — sensor response rarely perfectly linear
  • Ground-truth weighing: Weighbridge or calibrated chaser-bin scale as reference
  • Crop-specific calibration: Grain density differs between wheat, barley, canola, lupins
  • Each grain type requires own calibration
  • Ideally repeat each season as sensors drift with wear
  • Header verification: Width and overlap sensors must be verified
  • Width error compounds directly into area error across entire map
  • Consequence: Uncalibrated monitor can misreport yield by 10–20%
  • Error propagates into every zone-delineation decision from that map
Walk through practical calibration procedure. Stress importance of multi-point approach and crop-specific calibration.

Data Cleaning Pipeline: Points to Map

  • Header lag correction: Grain was cut seconds before sensor measurement
  • Without correcting for travel-time offset (function of speed and header-to-sensor distance)
  • Every point smeared in direction of travel by tens of metres
  • Start/stop artefacts: Header lift/lower and end-of-row deceleration produce spurious high/low values
  • Partial-width passes: Common at irregular WA boundaries, incorrect area assumption unless corrected
  • Statistical outlier filtering: Remove implausible values beyond thresholds
  • Spatial interpolation: Grid cleaned point cloud into continuous yield surface
  • Suitable for overlay with soil, imagery, prior-year maps
  • Skipping this pipeline: Single most common reason yield map "doesn't make agronomic sense"
Detail data cleaning steps required. Emphasize importance of complete pipeline for defensible maps.

Interpreting Yield Maps: Signal vs Noise

  • Key principle: Distinguish single-season from multi-year pattern before drawing conclusions
  • Single-season map heavily influenced by one-off events (isolated frost, unusual wet patch)
  • May not recur
  • Multi-year yield stability analysis: Overlay/classify several seasons
  • Identify zones that are consistently high, consistently low, or genuinely unstable
  • Reveals fundamentally more reliable picture
  • Practical payoff:
  • — Consistently low zone (fixed soil constraint) → permanent zone-based variable-rate strategy
  • — Unstable zone (season-dependent interaction) → season-adaptive, in-season sensing
  • Direct practical link back to diagnostic categories in Part 7
Present key principle for sound interpretation. Explain multi-year approach and its management implications.

Quality Sensors: Composition Beyond Quantity

  • On-header or post-harvest NIR protein sensors: Increasingly on WA combines
  • Allow real-time, spatially-resolved grain protein mapping
  • WA relevance: Protein determines wheat grading and price premiums
  • Differentiates Australian Premium White from Australian Standard White
  • Oil-content sensing: Equivalent role for canola
  • Agronomic insight combining yield and quality maps:
  • — High yield, low protein → N diluted by yield potential (not deficiency)
  • — Low yield, low protein → genuine nutrient or soil constraint
  • Very different management signals
  • Can only be made by overlaying quality on yield data
Extend monitoring from quantity to composition. Explain how quality and yield overlay reveals distinct management signals.

Part 9: Satellite and Aerial Remote Sensing at Scale

Platforms and trade-offs

Remote Sensing Platforms: Three Tiers

  • Proximal/handheld: Centimetre-scale points, immediate feedback
  • Not scalable beyond few sampling points without significant labour
  • UAV (drone) multispectral: Centimetre-decimetre resolution, full-paddock coverage in single flight
  • Flexible timing under operator control
  • Constrained by battery endurance and operator effort on large WA paddocks
  • 200+ hectare paddock requires multiple battery swaps
  • Satellite: Typically 10–30 m resolution, systematic free large-area coverage
  • Fixed revisit schedule, subject to cloud cover
  • Acquisition timing user cannot control
  • No platform dominates every dimension: Choice depends on question being asked
Compare three platform tiers. Emphasize trade-offs governing appropriate choice for different tasks.

Satellite Systems for Crop Monitoring

  • Sentinel-2 (ESA): 10 m resolution, free, ~5-day revisit from paired constellation
  • Accessed through Copernicus data archive, decision-support platforms
  • Landsat (USGS): 30 m resolution, free, continuously running archive extending decades
  • Valuable for multi-year yield and vegetation-index stability analysis
  • Commercial high-resolution (Planet): Finer spatial detail at subscription cost
  • WA limitation specific to Mediterranean climate: Cloud cover during wettest growing season
  • Precisely when in-season crop decisions matter most
  • Can leave weeks between usable cloud-free satellite images
  • Constraint proximal and UAV sensing do not share
Survey relevant satellite systems. Highlight WA-specific cloud limitation during critical crop monitoring period.

Choosing the Right Platform

  • In-season nitrogen topdressing decision: Needs fast, controllable timing
  • UAV or proximal sensing — waiting for cloud-free satellite pass could miss application window
  • Multi-year management-zone delineation: Well served by free, long-running satellite archives
  • Low cost and long record length matter more than fine spatial/temporal control
  • Fine-scale disease-patch confirmation: Whatever sensing layer flags anomaly
  • Ultimately requires ground-scouting visit to identify pathogen
  • Reinforces equifinality and ground-truthing lesson throughout lecture
  • Frame: Practical translation of research remote sensing into everyday WA agronomist decisions

Part 10: Synthesis — From Diagnosis to Action

Putting the scales together

Field Scouting Routine
